Satellite Orbit Types Explained

A clear guide to low Earth orbit, medium Earth orbit, geostationary orbit and polar orbits, their trade-offs, and the mission needs they serve.

← Back to the HFL Tech Journal

Orbit is one of the earliest choices in a satellite mission because it shapes coverage, latency, revisit time, communications, power needs and mission cost. There is no single “best” orbit: the right orbit depends on what the spacecraft must observe or deliver.

Low Earth orbit (LEO)

Low Earth orbit is relatively close to Earth, commonly used by Earth-observation spacecraft, crewed missions and many broadband-communications constellations. A spacecraft in LEO moves quickly relative to the ground, so an individual satellite sees a limited area at a time. Constellations, ground networks and careful orbit planning can help provide more frequent coverage.

LEO can offer useful imaging detail and relatively short communication paths, but satellites may need frequent handovers between ground stations or network satellites. Atmospheric drag is also more relevant than at higher altitudes, especially for lower orbits.

Medium Earth orbit (MEO)

Medium Earth orbit lies between LEO and geostationary altitude. Navigation satellite systems commonly use MEO because it can provide broad visibility from each spacecraft while balancing coverage and constellation size. The precise altitude and inclination depend on the mission architecture.

Geostationary orbit (GEO)

A satellite in a circular equatorial orbit with a period matching Earth's rotation can appear nearly fixed over one longitude. This is useful for continuous regional communications and weather monitoring. GEO spacecraft cover large areas, but the long distance introduces more signal delay and makes very high-resolution imaging more challenging than from lower orbits.

Polar and sun-synchronous orbits

In a near-polar orbit, the spacecraft passes over or near the poles as Earth rotates beneath it, allowing broad global coverage over time. A sun-synchronous orbit is designed so a satellite crosses a given latitude at approximately consistent local solar time. This repeatable lighting can help compare images across days and seasons, which is valuable for mapping land, vegetation, ice and environmental change.

How engineers choose

  • Mission objective: communications, navigation, weather, imaging or science.
  • Coverage and revisit: how often a region must be observed or served.
  • Latency and link budget: signal travel time, antenna gain, power and data rate.
  • Spacecraft constraints: propulsion, thermal control, radiation, power and lifetime.
  • Operations: ground-station access, collision avoidance and end-of-life disposal.
