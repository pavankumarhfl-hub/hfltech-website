Satellite Design Basics: Requirements, Subsystems and Verification

Satellite engineering begins with the mission objective. The spacecraft, payload, orbit, communications, power, thermal design and ground segment must work as one system, with verification planned from the start.

1. Define the mission objective

A satellite mission needs a specific purpose, such as collecting imagery, relaying communications or measuring an environmental variable. The objective shapes the orbit, payload, revisit requirements, data rate, lifetime and ground infrastructure. Without a defined mission, subsystem choices cannot be evaluated meaningfully.

2. Translate the mission into requirements

System requirements describe what the complete mission must achieve. They should be measurable, feasible and traceable to stakeholder needs. Examples include spatial or temporal resolution, data availability, pointing accuracy, operating lifetime, downlink capacity and limits on mass, volume and power. Each requirement should have an agreed verification method.

3. Understand the main spacecraft subsystems

  • Payload: the instrument or equipment that performs the primary mission.
  • Structure: supports equipment and carries launch and operational loads.
  • Electrical power: generates, stores and distributes energy to spacecraft loads.
  • Thermal control: keeps components within permitted temperature ranges.
  • Attitude determination and control: estimates and controls spacecraft orientation.
  • Command and data handling: manages onboard computing, commands and data.
  • Communications: carries telemetry, commands and mission data between spacecraft and ground systems.
  • Propulsion, where required: supports orbit changes, momentum management or end-of-life manoeuvres according to mission design.

4. Treat interfaces as first-class engineering work

Subsystems exchange mechanical loads, electrical power, data, timing, thermal energy and commands. An interface definition should specify connectors, protocols, limits, units, timing and failure behaviour. Many integration problems emerge at interfaces even when individual components pass their own tests.

5. Design verification alongside the hardware

Verification asks whether the system meets its specified requirements. Depending on the requirement, evidence may come from analysis, inspection, demonstration or test. A verification matrix connects each requirement to its method, acceptance criteria, responsible owner and result. This plan should evolve as the design matures.

6. Understand environmental testing

Spacecraft can experience vibration and shock during launch, vacuum, thermal cycling, electromagnetic interactions and radiation-related effects. The appropriate test programme depends on the mission, hardware class and applicable standards. Test levels, fixtures, instrumentation and acceptance criteria require qualified engineering review. A simulation or classroom prototype is not a substitute for qualification testing.

7. Keep mission readiness claims precise

A concept, CAD model, simulation, breadboard, engineering model, qualification article and flight unit are different maturity stages. A credible project description names the stage actually reached and the evidence available. This distinction is especially important for long-term initiatives, where planned capability must not be confused with a tested spacecraft or an operational service.
