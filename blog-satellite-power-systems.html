Satellite power systems: how spacecraft keep the lights on

A satellite needs dependable electrical power for its computer, sensors, radio, thermal controls and other subsystems. Its power system must balance generation, storage, distribution and demand across the full mission—not just in ideal sunlight.

Published 24 October 2026 · Engineering explainer

This article explains general spacecraft engineering principles. It is educational material, not a statement that HFL Astra has built or flown a satellite or validated a flight power system.

1. Where does satellite power come from?

Many Earth-orbiting satellites use photovoltaic solar arrays to convert sunlight into electrical energy. Array output depends on cell efficiency, illuminated area, pointing, temperature, degradation and the spacecraft's orbit. Some missions use other sources where mission needs justify them, but solar generation is common for many modern spacecraft.

2. Why batteries still matter

A satellite may pass through Earth's shadow or experience a temporary mismatch between generation and demand. Rechargeable batteries store energy during suitable periods and supply it when the arrays cannot meet the load. Battery sizing therefore considers eclipse duration, allowable depth of discharge, charge rates, temperature and ageing.

Key idea: A power budget is a mission constraint. Every additional sensor, radio transmission or heater has a cost in watts, energy, mass, thermal load and operational time.

3. Power conditioning and distribution

Solar arrays and batteries do not automatically deliver the exact voltage and current every subsystem requires. Power electronics regulate and route energy, protect circuits, monitor faults and help maintain stable supply. Engineers assess operating modes, peak demand, start-up surges, conversion losses and fault tolerance.

4. Build a realistic power budget

A first-pass budget lists each load and the mission mode in which it operates. Engineers estimate power, duty cycle, operating duration and uncertainty, then total the demand for each mode. They compare it with expected generation and storage under worst-case conditions and add justified margins. The result must be refined as hardware and mission assumptions become more concrete.

  • Nominal mode: normal sensing, computing and communications.
  • High-demand mode: instruments or transmitters operating together.
  • Safe mode: essential functions retained while non-critical loads are switched off.
  • End-of-life case: reduced array performance and battery capacity are considered.

5. Verification matters as much as design

Simulation and calculations are early steps, not substitutes for tests. A credible development process documents requirements, validates assumptions, tests power electronics and batteries safely, measures subsystem loads and checks fault responses. Space qualification requires mission-specific engineering and evidence.
